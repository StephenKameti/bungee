# Market Module: East Africa

This is a worked market module. Load it when the brief states Kenya, or another East African market, as the market. Treat Kenya as the fullest reference inside the region; where another East African market diverges from it, that is noted inline. When the brief names a different market entirely, do not reuse this file, build or load the matching one instead.

---

## Casting notes

Cast by context, never by stacked category. Do not write "Black Kenyan woman, late 20s." Write the setting and let it carry the specificity: "a woman with deep brown skin and short natural hair, photographed in a Nairobi apartment kitchen in morning light."

- Skin tone, hair texture and styling should read as everyday Kenyan, not runway or diaspora stock. Natural hair, braids, low cut fades and headscarves are all normal register, not a statement
- Household and shopper work is usually women led in the casting brief, but confirm with the spine rather than assume
- Trade and B2B work (distributors, retailers, sales reps) skews male in most FMCG briefs on the ground; do not default to this without checking the brief, and push back if it looks like a lazy assumption rather than a stated fact
- Groups of three or four read as a real household or friend group; groups of six or more read as stock casting
- Dress code follows occasion and class register stated in the spine: smart casual for urban middle class settings, uniform or work wear for trade and informal sector settings, kanga and kitenge as everyday wear for many women at markets, at home and on the coast as well as in festive settings, cast where the scene calls for them and never used as generic decoration

## Places and settings

Name the specific place, never "an African market" or "an African street."

- Nairobi estates and neighbourhoods carry distinct class and character signals: Kilimani and Lavington read upper middle class and leafy; Eastlands estates like Umoja or Buruburu read working and lower middle class, dense low rise housing, matatu culture visible; Kibera and similar informal settlements should only appear when the brief is explicitly about that context, handled with care and never as generic "poverty" set dressing
- Kitchens: small galley kitchens in urban apartments, gas cylinder cookers, open shelving, a jiko charcoal stove where the brief is rural or informal
- Retail: open air fresh produce markets (mama mboga stalls), small independent kiosks and dukas, mid size and large supermarket aisles, pharmacy counters
- Transport: matatus (the shared minibus system, usually vividly branded and decorated), boda bodas (motorcycle taxis), traffic on the main arterial roads out of Nairobi
- Outside Nairobi: Mombasa carries coastal, Swahili coast architecture, warmer and more humid light, different pace; Kisumu carries lakeside Luo cultural context; smaller towns and rural counties carry agricultural and market town settings, not a downgraded version of Nairobi
- Regional notes: Kampala (Uganda) has its own hill geography and boda boda density; Dar es Salaam and Zanzibar (Tanzania) carry a stronger Swahili coast and Islamic cultural register; Lusaka (Zambia) and Kigali (Rwanda) are calmer, more planned cityscapes with different traffic and street character. Do not reuse Kenyan street texture for these without checking

## Light and season

Kenyan light changes meaningfully across the year, and the brief's month matters.

- January to March: hot, dry, dusty light, long shadows, harsh midday sun
- April to June (the long rains): overcast, soft, diffused light, wet roads and greenery, puddles as a compositional device
- July to September: cooler, drier, clearer light, especially inland and at altitude (Nairobi sits at high elevation, the light is crisper than coastal Mombasa's haze)
- October to December (the short rains, running into the festive season): a mix of bright breaks and sudden rain, warm golden hour light in the lead up to Christmas

Coastal light (Mombasa, the wider coast) is warmer, hazier and more humid looking than Nairobi's highland clarity even in the same month.

## Language and local line rules, with examples

In Kenya, Uganda and Zambia the working language of business and advertising is English; in Tanzania it is Swahili (see Languages by country). In Kenya the local line is Swahili, often blended with Sheng, the urban youth vernacular that mixes Swahili, English and Kenyan slang.

- Blend naturally the way people actually speak: an English sentence with a Swahili beat, or the reverse. Put the Swahili on the emotional line and the English on the functional line
- Sheng is youth and urban register. Do not reach for it in a brief targeting 35 plus household shoppers or a formal B2B audience unless there is a specific reason
- Use words that live in kitchens, matatus and markets, not words that live in a dictionary
- Test the exact audience named in the spine: a line for a 45 year old shopper in a smaller town is not a line for a 22 year old in an affluent Nairobi neighbourhood, even though both are Kenyan
- Worked examples: "Twende kazi" (let us get to work) as an emotional Swahili beat paired with an English functional line, the shape used for an energy or action proposition. "Haina noma" (it is no big deal, it is fine) as a plain confidence line in Sheng register, the shape used for a reassurance proposition. Swahili carries genuine double meanings and wordplay that do not translate; when a brief's local line depends on one, gloss it in English rather than forcing an English pun into the same line
- Regional note: see Languages by country below. Uganda and Tanzania do not share Kenya's language order, and Zambia and Rwanda do not default to Swahili.

## Retail environment

- Supermarket format ranges from large modern chain supermarkets in urban malls down to mid size neighbourhood supermarkets and mini marts. Describe generically ("a large modern supermarket chain", "a neighbourhood mini mart") rather than naming a specific retailer
- Open air and informal trade is a major channel, not a fallback: fresh produce markets, kiosks, dukas, and roadside vendors carry a large share of shopper volume, especially outside major cities
- Pharmacies and chemists are a distinct, smaller format, relevant for health and personal care categories
- Point of sale materials must survive variable in store lighting, uneven shelf depth and handling by staff who are managing many brands at once. Shelf talkers and danglers need to read fast and cheap to produce
- Mobile money is a genuinely dominant payment behaviour at the till, more so than card payment in most retail contexts; if a KV shows a payment moment, a phone based mobile money tap or scan is more accurate than a card swipe

## Media landscape

- Chat app forwarding, on the dominant messaging app for the market, is a real world distribution channel for creative, often outperforming the feed post it was cropped from. Always test legibility at forwarded thumbnail size
- Billboards and street level OOH are viewed at speed from vehicles on arterial roads, or at a stationary glance in heavy traffic jams, both are real viewing conditions worth designing for
- Matatu and bus branding is a genuine, high frequency channel. Expect windows and doors to obscure roughly a third of any wraparound design
- Radio remains a strong reach medium outside major urban centres, relevant when a line needs to work read aloud, not just seen
- Television and social video both matter in urban markets; social reach skews toward messaging app status updates, short vertical video and mainstream social platforms alongside the more global ones

## Cultural moments, pointer only

Do not build a content calendar from memory. Research the current year's dates before using any of these, since several move on the lunar or agricultural calendar.

Recurring anchors to check for the live year: Jamhuri Day (independence, mid December), Easter, Ramadan and Eid (dates shift yearly), the back to school periods (January and May), the festive season running from late November through December, harvest and planting season markers relevant to agricultural categories, and county or regional cultural events where the brief's specific county is named.

## Languages by country

Set the language order from the country, not from Kenya.

- Kenya: English and Swahili are both official. Business and advertising run in English; the local line is Swahili, with Sheng for young urban audiences in Nairobi.
- Uganda: English leads in business and advertising. Swahili is official but is not the household language in Kampala. Luganda leads in Kampala and the central region, and other regions use their own languages (for example Runyankore in the west, Acholi or Lango in the north, Lusoga in the east). Write the local line in Luganda for Kampala and have a native writer check it.
- Tanzania: Swahili leads in daily life, media, business and advertising. Write Swahili first and English second, if at all. Coastal and Zanzibar Swahili differ from upcountry speech.
- Rwanda: Kinyarwanda leads, with English and French in business.
- Zambia: English leads in business. Bemba, Nyanja and other languages lead by region, not Swahili.
- Never reuse a Kenyan Sheng line unchanged in another country, and when a brief spans two countries write a local line for each.
