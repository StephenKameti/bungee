English gloss

Do your kaimati drink oil? The problem isn't the flour, it's the heat. Today, a small secret the mama lishe of Kariakoo have always known. Before the kaimati go in, drop one small blob of batter into the oil. If it sinks and sits, the oil isn't hot yet. If it rises fast, ringed with small bubbles, within three seconds, you're ready. Medium heat, not high: high heat burns the outside and leaves the inside raw. Don't crowd the pan; a few at a time so the oil keeps its heat. Turn them when golden, lift out, drain, then pour over cardamom sugar syrup. The result: light kaimati, a crust that crackles when you bite, and an inside soft as cotton. Try it today before futari and tell us how it went in the comments.

If oil catches fire in the kitchen, water is your enemy. It's something we'd rather not think about, but every cook should know it. Pour water on burning oil and the water flashes to steam, throwing hot oil and the fire itself up and everywhere. Do this instead, three steps. First, turn off the stove or gas if you can do it without putting yourself at risk. Second, cover the pan with its lid or a large metal tray so the fire runs out of air. Don't lift the pan or run outside with it. Third, leave it covered until it is completely cool. If the fire spreads, get out, close the door behind you and call the fire service emergency number, 114. Prevention is better than cure: never leave a pan of oil unattended, especially with children nearby, and never fill a frying pan more than half full of oil. The month of holidays is over and kitchens have worked hard. Save this post, and share it with one person who cooks in your home.
