## Section 1: Situation Analysis

Mobile money has already won the payment between a customer and a trader in both cities. The leg it has not won is the one before dawn, from trader to supplier, where cash still rules. The wallet category sells speed and convenience, but the woman walking into Marikiti at four in the morning is short of neither. What she lacks is proof. Supplier payments run on trust and credit: stock given today and settled tomorrow, recorded if at all in pencil in a supplier's exercise book that gets wet when it rains. A cash payment leaves no trace, and every market has its daily argument over a debt one side swears was paid. The product on sale is a wallet; the thing traders will buy is a witness.

Soko Wallet arrives as a second wallet in markets where one dominant mobile money network is a habit as old as the traders' phones. It cannot replace that habit and should not try. It has to win one job, paying suppliers, and own it. The brief's own arithmetic reframes the task: USD 150,000 for 30,000 paying traders is USD 5 a trader, which no amount of airtime buys. But a trader cannot pay digitally until her supplier accepts, and one wholesaler at Marikiti or Mabibo serves dozens of retail traders every morning. The objective is written as a trader number; the lever is a supplier number. Around 2,000 suppliers, each bringing fifteen of their traders, gets there.

The dominant networks own agent density and trust in both countries, and some Tanzanian wallets have at times shared earnings on balances with their users, a conversation this brief rules out completely. Banks court formal small businesses with merchant accounts open air traders do not want. Cash owns the dawn: it needs no signal, carries no charge for the receiver and asks for no explanation. The unclaimed territory is the trader's name. Nobody in the category has talked about reputation, which is the thing a market trader actually banks on.

Three forces shape the first half of 2027. Kenya votes on 10 August 2027 and the official campaign period opens on 29 May, so the last five weeks of the window land in campaign season: radio gets crowded and dearer, markets become rally grounds, and traders hold cash tighter in uncertain months. Traders in both cities read digital payments through a tax lens; Tanzania's 2021 levy on mobile money transactions, cut back after public outcry, and Kenya's repeated changes to taxes on mobile money, with the 2024 Finance Bill protests behind them, taught them that a digital payment can carry a cost and a visibility that cash does not. And Ramadan, expected from about 8 February to 9 March 2027, reshapes market hours in Dar es Salaam, while the long rains from March to May bring mud, supply shocks and the price swings that multiply payment disputes.

**For the creative team:** win the supplier at the gate and the trader follows; keep the name at the centre, and never say a word about tax, interest or returns.

## Section 2: Audience Architecture

**Tier 1, Primary: the retail traders.** Women in their thirties to fifties dominate fresh produce, fruit and fish retail, the mama mboga of every estate; men are more present in mitumba, cereals and hardware. In Nairobi they sell at Muthurwa, Kangemi, Githurai 45, Toi, Kawangware and City Market, and buy before dawn at Wakulima Market (Marikiti), at Gikomba for mitumba bales and at Burma for meat. In Dar es Salaam they sell at Buguruni, Temeke Stereo, Manzese, Mwenge and Ilala, and buy at Kariakoo, Mabibo, Tandale and the Feri fish market. The radio plays in the stall from opening to close. Voice notes beat text in the chama group in Nairobi and the vikoba or upatu group in Dar, and in the market association group. Short video fills the slow midday hour. Phones are a mix of smartphones and basic phones. A Nairobi mama mboga spends roughly KSh 2,000 to 5,000 on stock a day, a Dar trader roughly TSh 50,000 to 120,000 (validate in qual). **Trigger:** cash lost or snatched on the dawn walk, a fake note, a payment dispute, or the supplier saying he now prefers the wallet. **Brand relationship:** none; she already receives from customers on the dominant network. **Soko Wallet must prove:** the supplier sees her name the second she pays, it works on her phone, and the charges hold no surprises.

**Tier 2, Secondary: the suppliers.** Wholesalers at the market gates, farmers' agents and brokers (madalali), bale sellers at Gikomba and Kariakoo, fish buyers at Feri. Mostly men aged thirty to sixty. They count thick stacks by torchlight, carry the takings home on a matatu or a three wheeler taxi, and lose money to fake notes and short payments. Unusually for Tier 2, they are reached directly at the gate as well as through Tier 1, because this behaviour needs both sides to move. **Trigger:** a robbery, a fake note, a row with a good customer. **Soko Wallet must prove:** money lands instantly with the payer's name, cashing out is easy, and accepting costs nothing they did not expect.

**Tier 3, Amplifiers.** Market chairpersons (mwenyekiti wa soko) and market committees. Chama treasurers in Nairobi, vikoba and upatu leaders in Dar. The transporters who move stock between supplier and stall and know exactly who pays whom: mkokoteni pushers, boda boda riders, pick up drivers. The breakfast hosts on Swahili radio. **Soko Wallet must prove:** it works through the market's own leaders, openly, with ambassador fees agreed with the committee.

**For the creative team:** cast the trader and the supplier as two halves of one relationship, never as customer and vendor.

## Section 3: The Insight

**INSIGHT:** Before light I fold the day's stock money small and carry it through the market against my chest. My supplier gives me tomatoes on trust because he knows my name, and that name took me eleven years. If the money goes missing I can earn it back by Friday; my name, I cannot.

**ONE LINE BRIEF INSIGHT:** **"My name is my real capital, and every morning I carry it through the dark in cash."**
*In the room in Swahili: "Jina langu ndilo mtaji wangu halisi, na kila alfajiri nalibeba gizani kwa pesa taslimu."*

**WHY THIS INSIGHT WORKS:** For Tier 1 it names the fear under the fear: not losing cash but losing standing with the supplier who carries her on credit. For Tier 2 it is the same truth from the other side of the table, because a supplier's whole business rests on knowing whose name is good. It is exploitable across every channel because the market is full of scenes that carry it: the dawn walk, the torchlight count, the wet exercise book, the argument over a debt, the chalked name on a crate.

**For the creative team:** the hero is the name, and the dark is the enemy.

## Section 4: Campaign Platform

**CAMPAIGN NAME:** Jina Ni Mtaji *(Your Name Is Capital)*

**CAMPAIGN LINE (ENDLINE):** **Jina jema hung'aa gizani.** *(A good name shines in the dark.)*

**PLATFORM RATIONALE:** The proverb is known on both sides of the border, so the line arrives already trusted, and it describes the product literally: when a trader pays through the wallet, her name lights up on her supplier's phone before dawn. "Jina Ni Mtaji" makes reputation the asset, which keeps the whole campaign clear of interest, returns and credit while speaking the word traders use about their business every single day. No network can stand here, because networks sell sending money, and no bank can, because banks sell accounts. Only a wallet built for paying suppliers can make a trader's name the thing that travels.

**CAMPAIGN PROPOSITION:** Soko Wallet gives open air market traders in Nairobi and Dar es Salaam the pride and the peace of a name their suppliers can see, because every supplier payment arrives instantly with the trader's registered name and leaves a record both sides can check.

**TONE OF VOICE: Wa sokoni. Mkweli. Mcheshi.**
* **Wa sokoni (market true):** sounds like the gate at Marikiti or Mabibo at five in the morning, not like a banking hall.
* **Mkweli (plain spoken):** says exactly what happens to the money and nothing more, because traders have heard every promise before.
* **Mcheshi (warm humoured):** laughs with the market at its own arguments, never at the trader.

**For the creative team:** "mtaji" is only ever a metaphor for the name; it never sits beside a shilling figure or a verb of growth.

## Section 5: Creative Territories

### TERRITORY 1, SAA KUMI ALFAJIRI *(Four in the Morning)*
The dawn walk with the day's capital, and the morning it no longer has to happen.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion moves from tension to relief. We take the trader's most private ritual, folding the stock money small and hiding it against her chest before she walks into the dark, and we show the morning she walks in with her hands free while her name arrives at the supplier's stall ahead of her.

**HERO EXECUTION:** A 30 second film for social, messaging app sharing and the screens at the dawn desks, shot in two city versions from one grid (motion brief in Appendix B). Torchlight on folded notes; a fast walk past offloading lorries into Marikiti; a supplier holding a fake note to the bulb. Then the next morning: she taps her phone, and across the market his face is lit by her name on his screen. Copy line on screen: "Jina lako linafika kwanza." **Cast note:** Nairobi: Mama Neema, a woman in her forties with deep brown skin and short natural hair under a headwrap, a kanga tied over her dress as a market apron, gumboots for the mud; Mzee Kamau, a supplier in his late fifties in a grey work coat. Dar es Salaam: Bi Mwanaidi, a woman in her forties in a headscarf with a kanga tied as an apron; Bwana Hamisi, a supplier in an embroidered kofia.

**COPY LINES:**
* Swahili master: **Usibebe mtaji gizani.**
* English: Don't carry your capital through the dark.
* Nairobi Sheng (social and younger traders only): Usibebe doh gizani.
* Dar street (social and younger traders only): Usibebe mshiko gizani.

**BEST FOR:** Social and messaging app sharing, Tier 1 and Tier 2.

**WHY IT WORKS:** The dawn cash walk is near universal in both cities' markets and almost never spoken of, so seeing it on screen feels like being understood. The relief is physical: empty hands at four in the morning.

### TERRITORY 2, NILIKULIPA! *(I Paid You!)*
The market's oldest argument, ended by a phone.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is laughter. Every market has the same daily row: a trader swears she paid yesterday, the supplier swears she didn't, the exercise book is smudged, the whole row joins in. This territory turns that row into a radio comedy with recurring characters in each city, and every episode ends the same way: "Angalia simu." *(Check your phone.)*

**HERO EXECUTION:** A radio series of six 30 second episodes, three per city, each a different market and commodity (tomatoes at Marikiti, mitumba at Gikomba, meat at Burma; rice at Mabibo, fish at Feri, cloth at Kariakoo). Breakfast hosts pick up the catchphrase in live reads. **Cast note:** voice actors with the right city's register: Nairobi Swahili with a Sheng speaking young transporter as the comic third voice; Dar es Salaam Swahili with a Dar street speaking mkokoteni pusher in the same role.

**COPY LINES:**
* Swahili master: **Nilikulipa? Angalia simu.**
* English: Did I pay you? Check your phone.
* Nairobi Sheng: Ati sijakulipa? Cheki simu.
* Dar street: Eti sijakulipa? Cheki simu yako.

**BEST FOR:** Radio, Tier 1 and Tier 2, with the catchphrase carried into market signage.

**WHY IT WORKS:** Humour is how markets in both cities handle conflict, and a catchphrase that lets either side win an argument gets repeated at the stall. It makes the wallet the referee both sides trust.

### TERRITORY 3, UKUTA WA MAJINA *(The Wall of Names)*
Each market's good names, painted where everyone can see them.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is pride and belonging. At six markets, the entrance wall becomes the Wall of Names: every week, sign writers add the names of the traders who paid their suppliers through Soko Wallet that week, first name and stall number, opt in only. The walls fill across the half year, markets compete for the fullest wall, and Nairobi and Dar es Salaam trade friendly insults on breakfast radio.

**HERO EXECUTION:** Painted walls at Muthurwa, Kangemi and Githurai 45 in Nairobi, and Buguruni, Temeke Stereo and Tandale in Dar es Salaam, each headed "Majina Mema ya Soko la [market]." A weekly reveal at seven in the morning with the market chairperson, filmed for social, and the count read out on breakfast radio. **Cast note:** the traders themselves, photographed beside their own names, with signed consent.

**COPY LINES:**
* Swahili master: **Soko hili lina majina mangapi?**
* English: How many good names does this market have?
* Nairobi Sheng: Muthurwa iko na majina ngapi?
* Dar street: Buguruni kuna majina mangapi?

**BEST FOR:** Market activation and social, Tier 1 and Tier 3.

**WHY IT WORKS:** Market identity is fierce, and a public wall turns a private payment into standing in front of the people whose opinion matters most. A painted wall is also how markets in both cities already advertise, so it reads as the market's own.

**For the creative team:** three siblings, three feelings (relief, laughter, pride), one name at the centre of each.

## Section 7: Channel and Touchpoint Map

**MARKET ACTIVATIONS**
* Role in the campaign: recruit suppliers at the gate before dawn, then onboard their traders behind them; build the Wall of Names.
* Audience tier served: Tier 2 first, then Tier 1; run with Tier 3.
* Key message: Jina lako linafika kwanza.
* Format / spec: Meza ya Alfajiri (Dawn Desks) at seven supply markets, open 4:00 to 9:00 am, six days a week: Wakulima Market (Marikiti), Gikomba and Burma in Nairobi; Kariakoo, Mabibo, Tandale and Feri in Dar es Salaam. 60 Mabalozi wa Soko (market ambassadors), 36 in Nairobi and 24 in Dar, recruited from traders and transporters, each with a headtorch, apron and onboarding phone. Midday onboarding at eleven retail markets in the slow hour around saa saba (1:00 pm). Six Walls of Names. Supplier stall plates for every supplier who accepts.
* Timing: ambassador training 4 to 8 January; desks open 11 January; walls from 15 February; Dar desk hours shift for Ramadan.
* KPI: 2,000 suppliers accepting by 31 March (1,200 Nairobi, 800 Dar); 50,000 traders registered by 31 May; 30,000 paying by 30 June.
* Budget: USD 62,000.

**RADIO**
* Role in the campaign: make "Nilikulipa?" the market's catchphrase and send traders to the desks.
* Audience tier served: Tier 1 and Tier 2.
* Key message: Nilikulipa? Angalia simu.
* Format / spec: six 30 second episodes; breakfast host live reads with the weekly Wall of Names counts. Nairobi: Swahili mass market stations, breakfast and early drive. Dar es Salaam: Swahili commercial stations, breakfast. English stations are dropped (traders do not listen and the brief is Swahili first); vernacular stations in Nairobi are held back for phase two if qual shows Kikuyu, Luo or Luhya speaking traders are missed.
* Timing: 18 January to 28 May in Nairobi, front loaded before the campaign period opens on 29 May; 18 January to 30 June in Dar.
* KPI: 30 percent of desk registrations cite radio; prompted recall of "Nilikulipa" among market traders of 40 percent by April (market intercept).
* Budget: USD 33,000 (Nairobi 20,000, Dar 13,000).

**MESSAGING APP COMMUNITIES**
* Role in the campaign: carry each supplier's switch through the groups his traders already belong to.
* Audience tier served: Tier 1 and Tier 3.
* Key message: Kuanzia Jumatatu, supplier wetu anapokea kwa Soko Wallet. *(From Monday, our supplier accepts Soko Wallet.)*
* Format / spec: voice notes of 30 to 45 seconds, forward ready images and the 15 second film, shared by group admins who opt in; four community managers, two per city; a group code per admin. Legible at forwarded thumbnail size. Opt in only.
* Timing: from 18 January, each push timed to a supplier switching.
* KPI: 600 groups with an opted in admin; 35 percent of registrations arrive with a group code.
* Budget: USD 12,000.

**SOCIAL**
* Role in the campaign: give the campaign a face beyond the market, reach younger traders and transporters, and turn wall reveals into fame between the two cities.
* Audience tier served: Tier 1 (younger traders) and Tier 3.
* Key message: Usibebe mtaji gizani.
* Format / spec: the 30 second film and 15 second short vertical, image and community feed posts, weekly Wall of Names reveal films; geo targeted to the areas around the eighteen markets.
* Timing: always on, with peaks at launch and at each monthly wall reveal.
* KPI: 15 percent of registrations via social codes; cost per registration under USD 1.00.
* Budget: USD 15,000.

### Phasing

* **Lango (The Gate), 4 January to 14 February.** Ambassadors trained, desks open, suppliers first; radio starts 18 January; traders onboard behind each supplier who switches.
* **Jina (The Name), 15 February to 15 April.** Walls go up; Nilikulipa runs in both cities. In Dar, Ramadan (about 8 February to 9 March) moves desk hours around suhoor and the evening market. Long rains from March: the season of wet exercise books.
* **Soko kwa Soko (Market by Market), 16 April to 28 May.** Market against market, Nairobi against Dar, on radio and social. Union Day in Tanzania (26 April), Labour Day (1 May), Eid al Adha (about 16 May).

## Section 9: Strategic Sign Off

**THE CAMPAIGN IN ONE PARAGRAPH:**
Every morning before light, thousands of traders in Nairobi and Dar es Salaam fold the day's capital small, hide it against their chests and walk it through the dark to a supplier who trusts them by name. Jina Ni Mtaji speaks to those traders and to the suppliers on the other side of the table, and tells them that the thing they really trade on is their name. It says it in Swahili first, in the proverb both cities already know, on the radio that plays in every stall, in the groups where traders save together, and on the walls at the market gate. It turns paying a supplier from a risky walk into a moment of pride, when a name lights up a phone in the dark before its owner arrives. The behaviour it changes is the last cash habit in the market: 30,000 traders paying their suppliers through Soko Wallet by the end of June. Jina jema hung'aa gizani.

**THE BRIEF INSIGHT (REPEATED):** **"My name is my real capital, and every morning I carry it through the dark in cash."**

**THE CAMPAIGN LINE (REPEATED):** **"Jina jema hung'aa gizani."**
