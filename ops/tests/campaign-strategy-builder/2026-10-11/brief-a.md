## Section 1: Situation Analysis

Savings apps in Mexico sell a number: a rate, a goal, a projected balance at the end of the year. The rider is buying something else entirely, which is permission to have a bad day. And the category's favourite word is a regulated one. An electronic money institution may hold and move a customer's money, but it may not open savings accounts or promise returns, so "ahorro" and every rate headline belong to whoever holds the licence. The loudest lever in the category is one this brief may not be allowed to pull, and the audience does not want it anyway: a rider with three thousand pesos set aside does not care about yield, he cares that the week has a floor.

Fieldnote starts with no awareness among riders, an English name in a Spanish first market, and a cultural gap that is bigger than either. In Mexico City, saving is coded as something godínez do: salaried office workers with a quincena on the 15th and the last day of the month, a payroll card and a pension. Riders do not see themselves as savers; they see themselves as people who get by. The arithmetic sharpens the problem. USD 400,000 for 50,000 active savers is USD 8 per active saver, well below what paid acquisition usually costs a financial app. Paid media alone will not reach this number. Riders have to bring in riders, so this plan is built around the kerbs, the groups and the creators where riders already talk to each other.

Banks and digital banks fight over the same salaried customer with rate headlines and cashback. The delivery platforms pay out weekly and have started adding money features inside their own apps. Neither speaks to the uneven week. The real competitor is older and stronger: the tanda, the rotating savings circle run by someone everyone trusts, and the cash kept in a drawer at home. Tandas work because they are social and fixed. They fail riders for exactly the same reason, because a fixed weekly contribution does not fit an income that swings from fourteen orders to none. Nobody in the category owns the bad day.

Mexico's platform work reform, in force since mid 2025, made income swings official. A platform worker now gets full social security in the months net earnings clear the monthly minimum wage, and drops back to basic accident cover in the months they don't. By December 2025 about 206,000 workers a month were over that line, and roughly three in four of them were the same people month after month (IMSS pilot figures as reported in January 2026; refresh before the board). A rider can be covered in March and uncovered in April. The window opens straight into the cuesta de enero, the January squeeze after December spending, felt hardest by anyone without a year end bonus to soften it, and it closes as the rainy season starts in late May, when afternoon storms empty the streets and turn the bad day into weather.

**For the creative team:** sell the floor, not the yield; the bad day is the brand's to own.

## Section 2: Audience Architecture

**Tier 1, Primary: the riders.** Delivery and gig workers aged 22 to 35. Mostly men on 125cc and 150cc motorbikes, a growing number of women riders, and younger riders on bicycles. They live on the eastern and northern edge, in Iztapalapa, Gustavo A. Madero, Nezahualcóyotl, Ecatepec and Chalco, and they earn in Roma, Condesa, Polanco, Del Valle, Narvarte, Nápoles and Santa Fe. The earning map and the living map are two different cities, joined by an hour or more on Calzada Ignacio Zaragoza, Viaducto and Calzada de Tlalpan. The phone sits on the handlebar for the whole shift. In the waits between orders, outside restaurants, they watch short video. Rider group chats carry the news that matters: robberies, police checks, accidents, closed streets. Money arrives in pieces: a weekly platform payout, cash from cash orders, coins from tips. **Trigger:** a bad week (rain, a stolen phone, a repair bill, a suspended account) or a very good night with something worth keeping. **Brand relationship:** none yet. Most have a payout card and many are in a tanda; nobody has ever sold them saving as something for them. **Fieldnote must prove:** it fits money that arrives unevenly, and it is there in cash when the bad day comes.

**Tier 2, Secondary: the household.** La jefa (his mother), a partner, younger siblings, sometimes a family back in Puebla or Oaxaca that receives a transfer. Women in their forties and fifties carry the household budget in many of these homes. They live where the rider lives and they hear about money through him and through the family chat. **Trigger:** the week the rent was covered without a loan from a cousin. **Brand relationship:** reached only through Tier 1, through the end of shift screen he chooses to share. **Fieldnote must prove:** a bad week no longer becomes the family's problem.

**Tier 3, Amplifiers.** Rider creators who already post the "cuánto gané hoy" earnings video, a whole genre on the short video platforms. The admins of rider group chats and the organisers of rider collectives. The organisers of tandas. The mechanics at the talleres on Viaducto and Tlalpan where riders wait for repairs and talk money. **Trigger:** a format that makes them look sharper to their own followers. **Fieldnote must prove:** it respects the rider, never lectures him, and pays creators fairly and on time.

**For the creative team:** cast the rider as the smartest money manager in the city, because he already counts every peso.

## Section 3: The Insight

**INSIGHT:** Some nights I close the app with fourteen orders and feel rich; some afternoons it rains, nothing drops, and I'm counting coins for petrol. Everyone tells me to save, but saving is for people who know what Friday pays. I'd save on a normal day, if I ever had one.

**ONE LINE BRIEF INSIGHT:** **"Saving waits for a normal day, and my work never has one."**
*In the room in Spanish: "El ahorro espera un día normal, y mi chamba nunca tiene uno."*

**WHY THIS INSIGHT WORKS:** For Tier 1 it names the real blocker, which is not discipline or knowledge but the shape of the income, and it flips the shame of "I can't save" into the pride of "my work is harder than yours." For Tier 2 it explains, without blame, why the household budget lurches week to week. It is exploitable across every channel because every kind of day is a scene: the rain, the Mother's Day rush, the flat tyre, the stolen phone, the fourteen order Friday, the tanda turn that never comes.

**For the creative team:** never show a normal day; every execution is a good day or a bad one.

## Section 4: Campaign Platform

**CAMPAIGN NAME:** El Día Bueno *(The Good Day)*

**CAMPAIGN LINE (ENDLINE):** **Lo que guardas, te guarda.** *(What you keep, keeps you.)*

**PLATFORM RATIONALE:** If there are no normal days, the only honest promise is to let the good days look after the bad ones. "El Día Bueno" turns saving from a monthly duty into a reflex on the nights when money arrives, which is exactly when a rider feels able to give something up. The line uses one verb twice, guardar, to keep and to protect, so it works under any licence because it promises protection by the rider's own hand and no return from the brand. Banks cannot stand here because their products and their language are built on the quincena, and the platforms cannot stand here because they are the source of the uneven week.

**CAMPAIGN PROPOSITION:** Fieldnote gives Mexico City's delivery and gig workers a floor under the uneven week, and the calm of knowing a bad day won't take the whole week with it, because it sets aside a share of each good day as it is paid instead of asking for a fixed amount on a fixed date.

**TONE OF VOICE: Callejero. Exacto. Leal.**
* **Callejero (streetwise):** talks like the waiting kerb on Álvaro Obregón at nine at night, not like a bank branch on Reforma.
* **Exacto (exact):** real pesos, real orders, real streets, because riders count to the coin and spot a vague number instantly.
* **Leal (loyal):** on the rider's side against the bad day, never against the rider; no lectures about spending.

**For the creative team:** the line is the rider's own wisdom handed back to him; the brand never takes the credit.

## Section 5: Creative Territories

### TERRITORY 1, HOY SÍ CAYÓ *(Today It Came)*
The good night, celebrated, and a piece of it kept.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is joy. We live inside the rider's best nights, when orders drop one after another and the city feels like it is paying him back, and we make keeping a piece of that night feel like the victory lap, not the sacrifice. The good night gets its own ritual: el corte, the end of shift count, with a slice set aside before the helmet comes off.

**HERO EXECUTION:** A 30 second hero film for social, creator channels and in app (full motion brief in Appendix B). Night on Álvaro Obregón in Roma Norte, wet asphalt, restaurant light on the visor. Order after order lands on beat; three doorways in three neighbourhoods; a laugh under the helmet at the red light on Glorieta de Insurgentes. At a taco stand at one in the morning he opens Fieldnote and a slice of the night slides into his stack. Copy line on screen: "Hay noches que cae todo." **Cast note:** Toño, a man in his mid twenties with brown skin and a short fade, wiry from riding, in an unbranded black rain jacket and a scuffed full face helmet, his backpack free of any platform marks.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **Hoy sí cayó. Guarda un pedazo.**
* Street register: Hoy sí cayó la chamba. Aparta tantito.
* *English gloss: Today it came. Keep a piece.*

**BEST FOR:** Hero film and paid social, Tier 1, plus the in app end of shift card.

**WHY IT WORKS:** It asks for the saving at the one moment a rider feels rich enough to give some away, and it uses "cayó," the word riders already use for work that comes in. A savings brand meeting him at his peak, not his panic, is new in this market.

### TERRITORY 2, TARDE DE LLUVIA *(Rainy Afternoon)*
The bad day, met by the good days you kept.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is relief. When the rainy season breaks in late May, afternoon storms empty the streets and the orders stop. This territory turns those storms into the campaign's proof: digital screens near the delivery hubs switch creative the moment it rains, and the message lands on the exact afternoon a rider is sheltering under an overpass with nothing coming in.

**HERO EXECUTION:** Rain triggered digital out of home on Insurgentes Sur and at Glorieta de Insurgentes, fed by live weather data. Dry: a sunlit rider and "Hoy sí cayó. Guarda un pedazo." Raining: the same rider under the Viaducto overpass, wet, smiling at his phone, and "Llueve. Tu día bueno te cubre." Painted walls (bardas) by local sign painters near the waiting points carry the static version all season. **Cast note:** Mariana, a woman in her late twenties with deep brown skin and her hair in a tight braid under her helmet, rain cape over her shoulders, one of the women riders the category never shows.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **Llueve. Tu día bueno te cubre.**
* Street register: Aunque no caiga nada, tu varo guardado te hace el paro.
* *English gloss: It's raining. Your good day has you covered. / Even when nothing comes, the cash you kept has your back.*

**BEST FOR:** Out of home near delivery hubs, May to June, Tier 1; the rain message also runs as a weather triggered paid social layer.

**WHY IT WORKS:** It makes the benefit felt at the exact moment of need, which no salaried savings product can do, and Mexico City's predictable afternoon storms make the trigger reliable. The rider sees the brand being right in real time.

### TERRITORY 3, CUÁNTO GANÉ, CUÁNTO GUARDÉ *(What I Made, What I Kept)*
We take over the riders' own earnings video and add one question.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is pride with a competitive edge. Rider creators already post what they earned today, a format their followers watch for honesty and bragging rights, and we add a second number, what they kept, turning a show of income into a show of skill. Every week the creators compare the kept number, never the earned one, so the hero is the rider who managed money best, not the one who rode longest.

**HERO EXECUTION:** A creator series: 40 rider creators across the city post a 15 second earnings and keepings video every week for twelve weeks, built from one template (motion brief in Appendix B). Each opens on the creator at their own waiting kerb: "¿Cuánto gané hoy?" Then the twist: "¿Y cuánto guardé?" A monthly round up crowns "El Corte del Mes," the rider who kept the most consistently, with the prize taken only from the approved list and the contest rules cleared by legal before the first post. **Cast note:** the creators are real riders, cast for credibility with other riders rather than follower count, at least a third of them women, filmed on their own phones at their own spots.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **¿Cuánto ganaste hoy? ¿Y cuánto guardaste?**
* Street register: ¿Cuánto sacaste hoy, carnal? ¿Y cuánto apartaste?
* *English gloss: How much did you make today? And how much did you keep?*

**BEST FOR:** Creator partnerships and boosted creator posts, Tier 1 and Tier 3.

**WHY IT WORKS:** It borrows a format riders already trust and changes the status game inside it. Mexico City's rider culture is intensely peer driven and online between orders, so a new number to brag about spreads by itself.

### TERRITORY 4, LA TANDA DE UNO *(The Tanda of One)*
The tanda, rebuilt for an income that never arrives on schedule.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is knowing wit. Everyone in Mexico City has a tanda story (the turn that came too late, the organiser who disappeared, the week you couldn't make your share), and this territory tells those stories with affection and offers a tanda that bends to your week, where you are the only member and it is always your turn. It never mocks tandas or the people who run them; it only frees the rider from the fixed contribution.

**HERO EXECUTION:** A short comic social series and a set of street furniture panels. In one film, a group of riders on a kerb in Narvarte argue about whose tanda turn it is; one of them shrugs, opens Fieldnote and says "Yo ya cobré." *(I've already had my turn.)* Panel headline: "Una tanda donde siempre te toca." **Cast note:** three or four riders who read as real friends from the same block, mixed ages within the band, in their own work clothes.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **Una tanda donde siempre te toca.**
* Street register: La tanda donde nadie se pela con tu lana.
* *English gloss: A tanda where it's always your turn. / The tanda where nobody runs off with your cash.*

**BEST FOR:** Image and community feed social, street furniture panels, Tier 1 and Tier 2 (the jefas who run the household tandas).

**WHY IT WORKS:** It translates a new product into the most familiar saving behaviour in the city, so there is nothing to learn. Only a Mexican audience gets the joke at full strength.

**For the creative team:** four siblings, four feelings (joy, relief, pride, wit), and every one of them ends on the same line.

## Section 8: Campaign Executions Overview

### Hero executions

## Section 9: Strategic Sign Off

**THE CAMPAIGN IN ONE PARAGRAPH:**
Mexico City runs on riders who have never had a normal day: fourteen orders one night, none the next afternoon, and a savings industry built for people paid on the 15th. El Día Bueno speaks to the gig and delivery workers who carry the city's dinners across it, and tells them the one thing no bank has: that the good days can look after the bad ones. It says it in their own words and their own places, on the walls where they wait, in the earnings videos they already watch, and on the screens that change the moment it rains. It turns the end of every shift into a small ritual of keeping, and the rider's own count into a new kind of pride. The behaviour it changes is simple and hard: setting aside a piece of every good day, until 50,000 riders have a floor under their week. Lo que guardas, te guarda.
