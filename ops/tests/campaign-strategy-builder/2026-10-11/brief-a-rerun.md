# Fieldnote: El Día Bueno

**Campaign strategy document**

**Brand:** Fieldnote, a savings app for gig and delivery workers in Mexico City
**Campaign:** El Día Bueno *(The Good Day)*
**Campaign line:** **Lo que guardas, te guarda.** *(What you keep, keeps you.)*
**Date:** 11 October 2026
**Prepared by:** to be named (Assumption 1)

**Market:** Mexico City and its metropolitan area. No market module covers Mexico, so every local detail below is built from the brief and from research into the city's riders, media, regulation and 2027 calendar.
**Campaign type:** Behaviour Change, with Product Launch in support. Deepest build in Sections 2, 3 and 7; Sections 4, 5 and 8 strengthened for the launch.
**The brief:** 50,000 new active savers in six months. USD 400,000. January to June 2027. Channels open: social, creator partnerships, out of home near delivery hubs, in app. Mandatories: Spanish first; regulator approved claims only. Three insight options for the board.
**Language:** written in English for the board. Every consumer line is Spanish first. Spanish is both the business language and the everyday language of the market, so the master is written natively in Mexico City Spanish, with a street register variant for younger riders and an English gloss in italics.

### Assumptions to confirm

1. **Author.** The agency or author name for this title page.
2. **Licence.** Which licensed entity holds Fieldnote balances: a bank, a popular savings company (SOFIPO) or an electronic money institution. It decides whether "ahorro" can lead consumer copy, and which disclosure line closes every film and every board.
3. **Approved claims list.** Until compliance supplies it, consumer copy describes only what the rider does with his own money, as set out in Claims control below.
4. **Mechanic.** The rider sets a share of each payout aside automatically, can add cash, and can take money out when he needs it. Shown at 10 percent for illustration.
5. **Active saver.** An account with at least one deposit in the last 30 days and a positive balance on 30 June 2027.
6. **Account opening.** A light first level account, opened with a phone number and an ID photo in a few minutes. The funnel's verification ratio in Section 7 rests on it.
7. **Brand assets.** Logo files, real app screens and the brand palette. The palette in Section 8 is a proposed campaign palette.

### Claims control

The mandatory "regulator approved claims only" is turned into two lists before any copy is written. Every consumer line in this document has been checked against them, in Spanish, in street register and in the English gloss.

**Approved for use now:** the rider's own actions (guardar, apartar, separar un pedazo, "tú decides cuánto"), and, once Assumption 4 is confirmed, the mechanic itself ("lo ves en tu app", "lo sacas cuando lo necesites"). Every rate, reward, referral prize or protection claim goes to compliance and enters copy only from the approved list.

**Judgement call for compliance:** "te guarda" (keeps you, looks after you) is a figure of speech about the rider's own money, not a deposit protection claim. Money held with an authorised fintech carries no federal government guarantee, so if compliance reads the line as protection, the ready fallback is **"Los días buenos cuidan a los malos."** *(The good days look after the bad ones.)* It carries the same idea and promises nothing on the brand's behalf.

## Section 1: Situation Analysis

Savings apps in Mexico sell a number: a rate, a goal, a projected balance in December. The rider is buying something else entirely, which is permission to have a bad day. And the category's favourite word is a regulated one. An electronic money institution may hold and move a customer's money, but it may not open savings accounts or promise returns, so "ahorro" and every rate headline belong to whoever holds the right licence. The loudest lever in the category is one this brief may not be allowed to pull, and the audience does not want it anyway: a rider with three thousand pesos set aside does not care about yield, he cares that the week has a floor.

Fieldnote starts with no awareness among riders, an English name in a Spanish first market, and a cultural gap bigger than either. In Mexico City saving is coded as something godínez do: salaried office workers paid on the quincena, the 15th and the last day of the month, with a payroll card and a pension. Riders do not see themselves as savers; they see themselves as people who get by. The arithmetic makes the gap the whole problem. Some 912,000 people were registered in the national social security pilot for platform workers in its first six months; if the Valley of Mexico holds a quarter to a third of them, the city has roughly 230,000 to 300,000 app riders and drivers, so 50,000 active savers would be one in five of them, bought at USD 8 each. Paid media cannot buy that and riders alone cannot supply it. Riders have to be both the spearhead and the recruiters: every rider who keeps a piece of his night brings in another rider, a driver, or someone in his life who is also paid by the job.

Banks and digital banks fight over the same salaried customer with rate headlines and cashback. The delivery platforms pay out weekly and have started adding money features inside their own apps. Neither speaks to the uneven week. The real competitor is older and stronger: the tanda, the rotating savings circle run by someone everyone trusts, and the cash kept in a drawer at home. Tandas work because they are social and fixed, and they fail riders for exactly the same reason, because a fixed weekly share does not fit an income that swings from fourteen orders to none. Nobody in the category owns the bad day.

Mexico's platform work reform made the uneven week official. Since July 2025 a platform worker gets full social security in the months net earnings clear the monthly minimum wage, and drops back to accident cover in the months they don't; in December 2025, 206,521 people cleared that line, and 77 percent of those who cleared it between October and December were the same people month after month (IMSS pilot figures, reported January 2026). A rider can be covered in March and uncovered in April. The window opens into the cuesta de enero, the January squeeze after December spending, and runs through the federal midterm calendar: precampaigns from early January to 12 February, campaigns from April to 2 June and polling day on Sunday 6 June, months in which the painted walls riders pass every day become party territory. It closes as the rains set in from late May, when afternoon storms empty the streets and turn the bad day into weather.

**For the creative team:** sell the floor, not the yield; own the bad day, and make every saver bring in the next one.

## Section 2: Audience Architecture

**Tier 1, Primary: the riders.** Delivery and gig workers aged 22 to 35: mostly men on 125cc and 150cc motorbikes, a growing number of women riders, younger riders on bicycles, and around them a second ring of app drivers who live the same uneven week. They live on the eastern and northern edge, in Iztapalapa, Gustavo A. Madero, Nezahualcóyotl, Ecatepec and Chalco, and they earn in Roma, Condesa, Polanco, Del Valle, Narvarte, Nápoles and Santa Fe. The earning map and the living map are two different cities, joined by an hour or more on Calzada Ignacio Zaragoza, Viaducto and Calzada de Tlalpan. **Media:** the phone sits on the handlebar for the whole shift; in the waits outside restaurants they watch short video; rider group chats carry the news that matters, from robberies and police checks to accidents and closed streets. Money arrives in pieces: a weekly platform payout, cash from cash orders, coins from tips. **Trigger:** a bad week (rain, a stolen phone, a repair bill, a suspended account) or a very good night with something worth keeping. **Brand relationship:** none yet. Most have a payout card and many are in a tanda; nobody has ever sold them saving as something for them. **Fieldnote must prove:** it fits money that arrives unevenly, and the money is there when the bad day comes.

**Tier 2, Secondary: the household.** La jefa, his mother; a partner; younger siblings; sometimes a family in Puebla or Oaxaca that receives a transfer. Women in their forties and fifties carry the household budget in many of these homes, and many of them are paid by the job too: cleaning houses by the day, selling at the tianguis, sewing at home. They live where the rider lives and hear about money through him and the family chat. **Media:** the family chat, forwarded voice notes, short video in the evening. **Trigger:** the week the rent was covered without a loan from a cousin. **Brand relationship:** reached through Tier 1, first through the end of shift card he chooses to share, then as savers in their own right through his invitation. **Fieldnote must prove:** a bad week no longer becomes the family's problem.

**Tier 3, Amplifiers.** Rider creators who already post the "cuánto gané hoy" earnings video, a whole genre on the short video platforms. The admins of rider group chats and the organisers of rider collectives. The organisers of tandas. The mechanics at the talleres on Viaducto and Tlalpan, where riders wait for repairs and talk money. **Media:** their own channels and groups, which they run. **Trigger:** a format that makes them look sharper to their own followers. **Brand relationship:** none, and wary of brands that talk down to riders. **Fieldnote must prove:** it respects the rider, never lectures him, and pays creators fairly and on time.

**For the creative team:** cast the rider as the sharpest money manager in the city, because he already counts every peso.

## Section 3: The Insight

Three candidate insights, built in the same format. Option A is recommended and is the only one carried into Sections 4 to 9.

### Option A (recommended): no normal days

**INSIGHT:** Some nights I close the app with fourteen orders and feel rich; some afternoons it rains, nothing drops, and I'm counting coins for petrol. Everyone tells me to save, but saving is for people who know what Friday pays. I'd save on a normal day, if I ever had one.

**ONE LINE BRIEF INSIGHT:** **"Saving waits for a normal day, and my work never has one."**
*For the room in Spanish (strategy language, not consumer copy): "El ahorro espera un día normal, y mi chamba nunca tiene uno."*

**WHY THIS INSIGHT WORKS:** For Tier 1 it names the real blocker, which is not discipline or knowledge but the shape of the income, and it turns the shame of "I can't save" into the pride of "my work is harder than yours." For Tier 2 it explains, without blame, why the household budget lurches week to week. It is exploitable across every channel because every kind of day is a scene: the rain, the Mother's Day rush, the flat tyre, the stolen phone, the fourteen order Friday, the tanda turn that never comes.

### Option B: saving belongs to the godínez

**INSIGHT:** Saving is for people with a quincena, a desk and a boss who pays on the 15th. I'm not that guy; I'm the one who brings them lunch. If I started saving I'd be pretending to be someone I'm not.

**ONE LINE BRIEF INSIGHT:** **"Saving is for godínez, and I'm the one who brings them lunch."**

**WHY THIS INSIGHT WORKS:** It names an identity barrier that is real and very Mexico City, and it hands the creative a sharp comic contrast between the office and the street. It reaches Tier 2 through the family's own hopes for a son with a desk job.

### Option C: the tanda keeps me honest

**INSIGHT:** In the tanda I never miss my share, because twelve people would know by Sunday. Alone, nobody notices when I skip a week, so I always skip. I don't need a better app; I need someone watching.

**ONE LINE BRIEF INSIGHT:** **"I only save when someone would notice if I didn't."**

**WHY THIS INSIGHT WORKS:** It explains why the tanda has outlived every bank product in the city, and it points to a social product: crews, visible streaks and shared goals. It is strongest for Tier 3, whose authority comes from being the one who keeps the group honest.

### Recommendation

**Option A.** It names a barrier that is true for every rider on every street, and it hands the brand a truth the rider is proud of rather than a flaw to correct. Option B loses because it makes the rider wrong about who he is, so every execution would spend its energy persuading him to become someone else. Option C loses because its answer is a fixed social obligation, the same structure that already fails riders when the week turns bad; it lives on inside Territory 4 rather than as the platform.

**For the creative team:** never show a normal day; every execution is a good day or a bad one.

## Section 4: Campaign Platform

**CAMPAIGN NAME:** El Día Bueno *(The Good Day)*

**CAMPAIGN LINE (ENDLINE):** **Lo que guardas, te guarda.** *(What you keep, keeps you.)*
Compliance fallback, ready if the judgement call goes the other way: **Los días buenos cuidan a los malos.** *(The good days look after the bad ones.)*

**PLATFORM RATIONALE:** If there are no normal days, the only honest promise is to let the good days look after the bad ones. "El Día Bueno" turns saving from a monthly duty into a reflex on the nights when money arrives, which is exactly when a rider feels able to give something up. The line uses one verb twice, guardar, to keep and to look after, so the care comes from the rider's own hand and the brand promises no return and no guarantee. Banks cannot stand here because their products and their language are built on the quincena, and the platforms cannot stand here because they are the source of the uneven week.

**CAMPAIGN PROPOSITION:** Fieldnote gives Mexico City's delivery riders and gig workers a floor under the uneven week, and the calm of knowing one bad day won't take the whole week with it, because it sets aside a share of each good day as it is paid instead of asking for a fixed amount on a fixed date.

**TONE OF VOICE: Callejero. Exacto. Leal.**
* **Callejero (streetwise):** talks like the waiting kerb on Álvaro Obregón at nine at night, not like a bank branch on Reforma.
* **Exacto (exact):** real pesos, real orders, real streets, because riders count to the coin and spot a vague number instantly.
* **Leal (loyal):** on the rider's side against the bad day, never against the rider, and never a lecture about spending.

**For the creative team:** the line is the rider's own wisdom handed back to him; the brand never takes the credit.

## Section 5: Creative Territories

### TERRITORY 1, HOY SÍ CAYÓ *(Today It Came)*
The good night, celebrated, and a piece of it kept.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is joy. We live inside the rider's best nights, when orders drop one after another and the city feels like it is paying him back, and we make keeping a piece of that night feel like the victory lap, not the sacrifice. The good night gets its own ritual, el corte, the end of shift count, with a slice set aside before the helmet comes off.

**HERO EXECUTION:** The 30 second hero online film for social, creator channels and in app (motion brief 1, Appendix B). Night on Álvaro Obregón in Roma Norte, wet asphalt, restaurant light on the visor. Orders land on the beat; three doorways in three neighbourhoods; a laugh under the helmet at the red light on Glorieta de Insurgentes. At a taco stand at one in the morning he opens Fieldnote and a slice of the night slides into his stack. On screen: "Hay noches que cae todo." **Cast note:** Toño, a man in his mid twenties with brown skin and a short fade, wiry from riding, in an unbranded black rain jacket and a scuffed full face helmet, his backpack free of any platform marks.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **Hoy sí cayó. Guarda un pedazo.**
* Street register: Hoy sí cayó la chamba. Aparta tantito.
* *English gloss: Today it came. Keep a piece.*

**BEST FOR:** The hero film and paid social, Tier 1, plus the in app end of shift card.

**WHY IT WORKS:** It asks for the saving at the one moment a rider feels rich enough to give some away, in "cayó," the word riders already use for work that comes in. A savings brand meeting him at his peak, not his panic, is new in this market.

### TERRITORY 2, TARDE DE LLUVIA *(Rainy Afternoon)*
The bad day, met by the good days you kept.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is relief. When the rains arrive in late May, afternoon storms empty the streets and the orders stop, and this territory turns those storms into the campaign's proof. Digital screens near the delivery hubs switch creative the moment it rains, so the message lands on the exact afternoon a rider is sheltering under an overpass with nothing coming in.

**HERO EXECUTION:** Rain triggered digital out of home on Insurgentes Sur and at Glorieta de Insurgentes, fed by live weather data. Dry: a sunlit rider and "Hoy sí cayó. Guarda un pedazo." Raining: the same rider under the Viaducto overpass, wet, grinning at her phone, and "Llueve. Hoy invitan tus días buenos." Painted walls (bardas) by local sign painters near the waiting points carry the static version all season. **Cast note:** Mariana, a woman in her late twenties with deep brown skin and her hair in a tight braid under her helmet, rain cape over her shoulders, one of the women riders the category never shows.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **Llueve. Hoy invitan tus días buenos.**
* Street register: Aunque no caiga nada, tu varo guardado te hace el paro.
* *English gloss: It's raining. Today your good days are buying. / Even when nothing comes, the cash you kept helps you out.*

**BEST FOR:** Out of home near delivery hubs from 17 May to 30 June, Tier 1; the rain message also runs as a weather triggered paid social layer.

**WHY IT WORKS:** It makes the benefit felt at the exact moment of need, which no salaried savings product can do, and the city's reliable afternoon storms make the trigger dependable. "Hoy invito yo" is how Mexico City says "my treat," so the good days picking up the bill reads as warmth, not finance.

### TERRITORY 3, CUÁNTO GANÉ, CUÁNTO GUARDÉ *(What I Made, What I Kept)*
We take over the riders' own earnings video and add one question.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is pride with a competitive edge. Rider creators already post what they earned today, and we add a second number, what they kept, turning a show of income into a show of skill. Each week the creators compare the kept number, never the earned one, so the hero is the rider who managed money best, not the one who rode longest.

**HERO EXECUTION:** A creator series: 40 rider creators across the city post a 15 second "what I made, what I kept" video every week for twelve weeks, built from one template (motion brief 2, Appendix B). Each opens on the creator at their own waiting kerb: "¿Cuánto gané hoy?" Then the twist: "¿Y cuánto guardé?" A monthly round up names "El Corte del Mes," the rider who kept most consistently, and paints their name on that month's barda; no cash prize enters unless it is on the approved claims list and the contest rules are cleared. **Cast note:** the creators are real riders, cast for credibility with other riders rather than follower count, at least a third of them women, filmed on their own phones at their own spots.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **¿Cuánto ganaste hoy? ¿Y cuánto guardaste?**
* Street register: ¿Cuánto sacaste hoy, carnal? ¿Y cuánto apartaste?
* *English gloss: How much did you make today? And how much did you keep?*

**BEST FOR:** Creator partnerships and boosted creator posts, Tier 1 and Tier 3.

**WHY IT WORKS:** It borrows a format riders already trust and changes the status game inside it. Rider culture in the city is intensely peer driven and online between orders, so a new number to brag about spreads by itself, and a name on a wall is worth more to a rider than a voucher.

### TERRITORY 4, LA TANDA DE UNO *(The Tanda of One)*
The tanda, rebuilt for an income that never arrives on schedule.

**THE IDEA:** The emotion is knowing wit. Everyone in Mexico City has a tanda story, the turn that came too late, the organiser who vanished, the week you couldn't make your share, and this territory tells those stories with affection before offering a tanda that bends to your week, where you are the only member and it is always your turn. It never mocks tandas or the people who run them; it only frees the rider from the fixed share.

**HERO EXECUTION:** A short comic social series and a set of street furniture panels. In the lead film, riders on a kerb in Narvarte argue about whose tanda turn it is; one shrugs, opens Fieldnote and says "Yo ya cobré." *(I've already had my turn.)* A Candelaria edit lands on Tuesday 2 February, the day whoever found the figure in the January rosca owes everyone tamales: fixed dates are for tamales, not for riders. Panel headline: "Una tanda donde siempre te toca." **Cast note:** three or four riders who read as real friends from the same block, mixed ages within the band, in their own work clothes.

**COPY LINES:**
* Spanish master: **Una tanda donde siempre te toca.**
* Street register: Tanda sin cuota fija, pa' la chamba que no es fija.
* *English gloss: A tanda where it's always your turn. / A tanda with no fixed share, for work that's never fixed.*

**BEST FOR:** Image and community feed social, street furniture panels, Tier 1 and Tier 2 (the jefas who run the household tandas).

**WHY IT WORKS:** It translates a new product into the most familiar saving behaviour in the city, so there is nothing to learn. Only a Mexican audience gets the joke at full strength.

**For the creative team:** four siblings, four feelings (joy, relief, pride, wit), and every one of them ends on the same line.

## Section 7: Channel and Touchpoint Map

**CREATOR PARTNERSHIPS**
* Role in the campaign: drive trial through trusted riders and start the "what I kept" conversation.
* Audience tier served: Tier 1 and Tier 3.
* Key message: ¿Cuánto ganaste hoy? ¿Y cuánto guardaste?
* Format / spec: 40 rider micro creators on a twelve week weekly series (15 seconds, 9:16, one template), plus 4 larger rider creators for launch and the May peak. Every post labelled #Publicidad; every creator's numbers real.
* Timing: seeding from Monday 4 January; weekly series Monday 15 February to Sunday 9 May; peak posts around Monday 10 May.
* KPI: 45,000 installs from creator codes at USD 2.33 each or less; at least 40 percent of creator installs reaching a first deposit.
* Budget: USD 105,000, 26.25 percent of the total.

**PAID SOCIAL**
* Role in the campaign: build reach around the hub zones and the home neighbourhoods, and retarget riders who installed but have not set anything aside.

## Section 9: Strategic Sign Off

**THE CAMPAIGN IN ONE PARAGRAPH:**
Mexico City runs on riders who have never had a normal day: fourteen orders one night, none the next afternoon, and a savings industry built for people paid on the 15th. El Día Bueno speaks to the gig and delivery workers who carry the city's dinners across it, and tells them the one thing no bank has: that the good days can look after the bad ones. It says it in their own words and their own places, on the walls where they wait, in the earnings videos they already watch, and on screens that change the moment it rains. It turns the end of every shift into a small ritual of keeping, and the rider's own count into a new kind of pride he shares with the people around him. The behaviour it changes is simple and hard: setting aside a piece of every good day, until 50,000 people who are paid by the order and the job have a floor under their week. Lo que guardas, te guarda.
